# Clifford and Non-Clifford Gates

## Clifford gates

A Clifford gate maps Pauli operators to Pauli operators. If you conjugate a Pauli (X, Y, Z, or a tensor product of them) by a Clifford gate U, you get another Pauli, possibly with a sign:

  U P U† = ±P′

For example:

- **H** swaps X and Z: H X H = Z and H Z H = X.
- **S** sends X to Y and leaves Z unchanged.
- **CNOT** spreads Paulis across qubits. X on the control becomes X⊗X, and Z on the target becomes Z⊗Z.

The whole Clifford group is generated by **H, S and CNOT**. Every Clifford circuit, on any number of qubits, can be built from those three. The group also includes X, Y, Z, S†, CY, CZ and SWAP.

### Why they matter

1. **They are classically easy to simulate.** This is the Gottesman–Knill theorem. A state made from |0…0⟩ by Clifford gates is a *stabilizer state*. You can describe it by tracking how the gates transform n Pauli generators, which takes about n² bits instead of 2ⁿ amplitudes. Simulating such a circuit and measuring it in the Pauli basis is polynomial. Bell states, GHZ states, teleportation and the encoders for most error-correcting codes are Clifford circuits. Entanglement alone does not give a quantum speedup.
2. **They are cheap in fault-tolerant hardware.** In most error-correcting codes, such as the surface code, Clifford gates can be done transversally or with lattice surgery, at fairly low cost.

## Non-Clifford gates

A non-Clifford gate is any gate outside that group. It maps at least one Pauli to a superposition of Paulis. Common examples:

- **T** = diag(1, e^{iπ/4}), the "π/8 gate". T X T† = (X + Y)/√2, which is not a single Pauli.
- **Toffoli (CCX)** and **CCZ**.
- **Rotations with general angles**: Rx(θ), Ry(θ), Rz(θ), P(θ), RZZ(θ) and so on. They are Clifford only when θ is a multiple of π/2. For example, P(π/2) = S, and RZZ(π/2) is Clifford up to a global phase.

### Why they matter

- **Universality.** Clifford gates alone are not universal. Adding any one non-Clifford gate is enough. **Clifford + T** is the standard universal gate set: any unitary can be approximated to arbitrary precision (Solovay–Kitaev).
- **They are where the quantum advantage comes from**, in the Gottesman–Knill sense. Classical simulation cost grows exponentially with the number of non-Clifford gates, not with the qubit count. Simulators that exploit this have a cost that scales with the circuit's T-count.
- **They are expensive to run fault-tolerantly.** A T gate usually needs *magic state distillation*: preparing many noisy copies of the state T|+⟩, distilling a cleaner one, and teleporting it into the circuit. This is why resource estimates for algorithms like Shor's are often given as a **T-count** or **Toffoli count**.

## In this app's palette

| Clifford | Non-Clifford (in general) |
|---|---|
| H, X, Y, Z, S, CX, CY, CZ, SWAP | Toffoli / multi-control CX, CY, CZ; P, RX, RY, RZ, RZZ, RXX, RYY at angles that aren't multiples of π/2 |

You can see the difference in the app. Build a Bell or GHZ state, which uses only Clifford gates, and the amplitudes stay in a small set: 0, ±1/√2ⁿ, ±i/√2ⁿ. Add an RY(θ) with an arbitrary θ and the amplitudes take continuous values. A stabilizer description can't capture those.
